\begin{abstract}
I competed in both GLEE tracks: as \textsc{7aidara\_Gamma}, a deterministic,
LLM-free agent that finished \tbf{6th in the agent track} (2360.8) over
\tbf{264{,}432 games}, and by hand, where I finished \tbf{2nd in the human
track}. The three policies are ${\approx}2{,}300$ lines of Python behind 114
parameters, with no learned model and no online adaptation. My central finding is
methodological and negative: \tbf{within the windows a competitor actually gets,
GLEE's percentile-Elo could not resolve the effects I was trying to produce}.
Five \emph{byte-identical} builds, polled simultaneously over ${\approx}3{,}000$
games each, differed by up to 88 rating points overall and 293 within a family
while their \emph{behaviour} differed by ${\le}0.010$ of the pot; separating
policies 0.25 points per game apart at $3\sigma$ needs
${\approx}5{,}600$--$6{,}700$ games \emph{per arm}. Across 30+ interventions and
49 runs only \emph{strictly dominated} fixes replicated, every tuning arm
measured nothing, and one unmeasured arm shipped fleet-wide cost 100--385
negotiation points per agent. Much of what the policies rely on is documented
nowhere and had to be recovered from play: the valuation pool, a round cap paying
both sides nothing, and the rating's update rule. I give four offline techniques
that measured what the leaderboard could not, and a survivorship failure mode
that corrupted four analyses. Playing both tracks also
yields a well-matched \emph{observational} human-machine comparison: agent and
human read seller messages with no difference I can detect (equal-cell hit rates
0.715 vs.\ 0.722, 95\% CI $[-0.050,+0.035]$), separating almost entirely on the
discipline to decline.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

GLEE~\cite{shapira2024glee}, in the lineage of automated negotiation
competitions~\cite{anac2011,baarslag2014decoupling}, evaluates agents in three economic
families: \emph{bargaining} (alternating-offer division with discounting),
\emph{negotiation} (bilateral trade under private valuations~\cite{myerson1983}) and
\emph{persuasion} (a 20-round sender--receiver game). Configurations come from a
grid of 960 combinations, and payoff is scored not in dollars but as a
\emph{percentile} against every payoff earned on the same configuration in the
same role, adjusted for opponent strength:
$r_{\text{game}} {=} 2000 + 8000(\text{pct}{-}0.5)$,
$\Delta R {=} \eta(r_{\text{game}}{-}R)$, $\eta \to 0.002$.

Two consequences shaped every decision. A \$0 no-deal sits at the bottom of that
scale, so closing a mediocre deal strictly dominates holding out and missing. And
the step size I recovered from my own rating deltas, $\eta{=}0.002$, implies an
EMA half-life of ${\approx}346$ games \emph{per family}: the rating is a smoothed,
lagged, opponent-contaminated statistic, and it is the only feedback the platform
gives.

I built a deterministic agent rather than an LLM one for three reasons. Percentile
scoring over a known parametric family rewards a correct decision rule; the
free-text channel showed no detectable effect (\S\ref{sec:ledger}); and determinism buys
what mattered most, namely that \tbf{a logged game replays to exactly the same
decisions}, which made offline evaluation possible once I found that the
leaderboard could not resolve the effects examined here.

One theme runs through what follows: \tbf{most of what the agent exploits is
undocumented and had to be inferred from play}, so every such fact is stated with
the evidence that recovered it. I contribute the agent (\S\ref{sec:agent}), a
measurement of the competition's noise floor and the sample it implies
(\S\ref{sec:noise}), four offline techniques that measure strategy changes
without the leaderboard (\S\ref{sec:measure}), a ledger of what replicated and
what cost me places (\S\ref{sec:ledger}), the required behaviour analysis
(\S\ref{sec:aba}), and an observational same-author baseline facing the same
configurations by hand (\S\ref{sec:human}).
