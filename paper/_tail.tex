\section{Additional methods and limits}
\label{app:lim}
\textbf{Aggregate inference for declined products.} Let $p$ be the prior
probability of high quality in a persuasion cell, $b$ the purchased fraction,
and $h$ the observed high-quality rate among purchases. The population identity
$p=bh+(1-b)q_{\rm declined}$ motivates
\[
\widehat q_{\rm declined}=\frac{p-\widehat b\,\widehat h}{1-\widehat b},
\qquad \widehat b<1.
\]
This uses the prior in expectation, not the realized total number of high-quality
units in a finite sample. It estimates an aggregate rate and cannot recover the
quality of a particular declined product. Near-complete purchasing makes the
estimate unstable; sampling variation can place it outside $[0,1]$. At $b=0$
it supplies no information beyond the prior. It should not be used as evidence
about a single hidden round or as a precision estimate without uncertainty.

\textbf{Simulation and memory.} A local bargaining simulator fitted to 15,788
games and eight adversarial archetypes reproduced per-cell median settlement
rounds but overestimated payoff by approximately 0.03 of the pot. It was used
for candidate screening rather than as evidence of a live treatment effect.
Opponent profiles showed split-half correlation $r=0.786$, but only 19
opponent-by-cell profiles had at least 25 purchases in each half. Identity was
withheld in 49.9\% of games, and no quality was revealed in 42,616 unbought
rounds. Purchase selection and sparse profiles limit the conclusions for
cross-game opponent modeling~\cite{baarslag2016survey}.

\textbf{Operational versioning.} Experimental flags written into a working
tree, entry points outside a copied package and abrupt mid-game termination
can make intended and deployed behavior differ. A reference build passing
tests does not establish that every concurrent slot ran that build. Exact
replay is tied to the logged cohort and policy version.

\section{Aggregation and outcome-dependent selection}
\label{app:simpson}
A candidate rule used whether a seller had previously declined an opportunity
to sell. Pooled across fifteen $(m,p)$ cells, later low-quality rates were
0.285 / 0.287 / 0.286 / 0.315 for sellers declining 0 / 1 / 2 / 3--4 of their
first four opportunities. Within $(m=1.20,p=0.80)$, the corresponding values
were 0.160 / 0.102 / 0.081 / 0.069 (cell sample $n=9,932$), against an immediate
break-even low-quality rate $1-1/m=0.167$. This illustrates how configuration
mixture can mask a within-cell pattern; it is not evidence of a uniform reversal
in every cell. The rule was not deployed.

Separate analyses were affected by outcome-dependent inclusion: deal rates
computed only among closed deals, quality estimates conditional on purchases,
and abandonment estimates from logs missing a completed final turn. These are
selection problems, distinct from aggregation over cells. One persuasion arm
also changed from $-3.11\sigma$ to $-0.82\sigma$ to $+3.02\sigma$ when the
minimum-count filter admitted 3, 33 or 44 cells. This sensitivity motivates
specifying inclusion rules before inspecting outcomes and treating retrospective
screening statistics as exploratory.

\section{Rating-model and reproducibility boundaries}
\label{app:measurement}
An idealized independent two-arm comparison with equal variance $\sigma^2$
and equal sample size $n$ has standard error $\sigma\sqrt{2/n}$. Requiring an
effect $\Delta$ to equal three standard errors gives
$n=18\sigma^2/\Delta^2$. Earlier screening applied this arithmetic to
per-game rating deltas, whose observed standard deviations were
4.84 / 4.42 / 4.44 over 120,023 joined games. At $\Delta=0.25$, it yields
roughly 5,600--6,700 observations per arm using unrounded estimates. This is
an IID sensitivity illustration, not a validated policy-strength detection
bound: deltas depend on current ratings and telescope over a run, and the
criterion does not specify statistical power. Fixed-reference game scores or
properly reconstructed game targets, with time and configuration controls,
would provide a better-defined comparison.

Similarly, a fitted zero rating-delta crossing is not generally the median of
the current payoff distribution. Rating history, opponent strength and a
changing reference pool enter that relationship. Comparisons with equilibrium
predictions additionally require the correct initial seat and horizon; an
infinite-horizon proposer share must not be treated as the equilibrium payoff
for all seats and finite horizons.

For a constant-$\eta$ EMA, perturbing one game target by $d$ changes the rating
by $\eta d(1-\eta)^k$ after $k$ later games if those later targets remain fixed.
The recorded sum of abandonment deltas therefore cannot be interpreted as a
final-standing effect. A persistent stream of abandonments and a finite cluster
near stopping time are different estimands; no precise persistent-cost estimate
is claimed here.

The public release includes policy code, tests and analysis scripts. Its scripts
require joined game records and human-history exports that are not distributed
alongside the code because they include opponents' transcripts. Organizers have
indicated plans to release game records, but local deployment flags, rating-poll
snapshots, filter choices and joins are additional provenance requirements.
The released method is inspectable; the empirical results are not yet a
one-command reproduction. Tables retain their analysis-specific cohorts, and
aggregate counts should not be substituted for the denominators of individual
comparisons.
